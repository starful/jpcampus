## What to Download Before You Even Board Your Flight

Japan experiences roughly 1,500 earthquakes a year, plus an active typhoon season from June through October. The good news: Japan has some of the most sophisticated disaster-alert infrastructure in the world, and most of it is now available in English. The bad news: if you wait until you're already feeling the floor shake to search the app store, you've lost precious seconds — and seconds matter when an earthquake early warning only gives you 5 to 30 seconds of lead time. This guide walks through exactly which apps to install, how to configure them correctly, and what to actually do when they go off.

## Earthquake Early Warning Apps: Your 5-30 Second Lifeline

Japan's Earthquake Early Warning (EEW) system, run by the Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA), detects the initial P-wave of an earthquake near its epicenter and pushes out alerts before the stronger S-wave (the one that actually shakes buildings) arrives. Depending on your distance from the epicenter, you get anywhere from 5 to 30 seconds of warning — enough time to drop, cover your head, and move away from windows or heavy furniture.

| App Name | English Support | Cost | Key Feature | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Yurekuru Call (ゆれくるコール) | Partial (alerts in Japanese, UI partially translatable) | Free | Fast P-wave-based push alerts | Most widely used by long-term residents |
| NHK World-Japan | Full English | Free | Earthquake, tsunami, and weather alerts with English voice/text | Best for overall news context |
| Safety tips (by Japan Tourism Agency) | Full English, plus Chinese/Korean | Free | Earthquake, tsunami, typhoon, and eruption alerts | Designed specifically for foreign residents/tourists |
| Yahoo! Japan Disaster Prevention (Yahoo!防災速報) | Japanese only | Free | Hyperlocal alerts by registered address | Very accurate but requires basic Japanese |
| Japan Official Travel App | Full English | Free | Combines alerts with travel/transit info | Good secondary backup |

**Priority install order:** Safety tips first (built for exactly your situation), NHK World-Japan second (adds broader context and live English broadcasts during major events), and Yurekuru Call third if you're comfortable navigating a mostly-Japanese interface for faster alerts.

### How to Set Up Notifications Correctly

A disaster app you forgot to configure is worse than no app at all, because you'll assume you're covered. Do this immediately after installing:

1. **Enable location services** set to "Always" or "While Using" — most apps need this to determine if an earthquake near you is strong enough to warrant an alert.
2. **Turn off battery optimization / Low Power Mode restrictions** for the app on Android, since aggressive battery savers can delay or block push notifications.
3. **Set notification priority to "Critical" or "Override Do Not Disturb"** in your phone settings — earthquake alerts should never be silenced, even at night.
4. **Register your actual residential address**, not just your current GPS location, in apps that allow it (like Yahoo! Bōsai Sokuhō). This ensures alerts fire even if you leave your phone on a desk in another room.
5. **Test the alert sound** once in settings so you recognize it. The JMA warning tone is a distinctive two-note chime — once you've heard it, your brain reacts faster next time.

## Typhoon and Severe Weather Tracking

Typhoon season brings a different kind of threat: it's slower-moving but can cause flooding, landslides, and days of transit disruption. Unlike earthquakes, you usually have 24-72 hours of advance notice, so the apps here are about planning rather than split-second reaction.

| App Name | Best For | English Support | Unique Feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| NHK World-Japan | Typhoon tracking maps | Full English | Live storm path visualization |
| Windy.com | Detailed wind/rain forecasting | Full English | Great for deciding whether to travel |
| tenki.jp | Hyperlocal Japanese forecasts | Japanese only (visuals are usable without text) | Most trusted by locals for precision |
| JMA official site/app | Government-issued warnings | Partial English | Official flood/landslide warning levels |
| Safety tips | All-in-one alert hub | Full English | Combines typhoon with earthquake/tsunami alerts |

**Understanding Japan's warning levels** is more useful than just checking rain percentages. Japan uses a 5-level disaster alert scale:

- **Level 1-2**: Stay informed, no action needed yet.
- **Level 3**: Elderly and those needing extra time should consider evacuating.
- **Level 4**: Evacuate — this is the level most residents should act on.
- **Level 5**: Disaster already occurring; take whatever protective action is still possible.

Safety tips and NHK World translate these levels into plain English, which standard Japanese weather apps often don't.

## Emergency Contact and Medical Help Apps

Calling 119 (fire/ambulance) or 110 (police) during a crisis is stressful enough without a language barrier. These tools bridge that gap.

- **Japan Helpline**: A 24/7 multilingual hotline (not an app in the traditional sense, but save the number: 0570-000-911) that can conference-call you with emergency services and translate in real time.
- **AMDA International Medical Information Center**: Offers phone consultations in English, Chinese, Korean, Spanish, Thai, and more, helping you find a nearby hospital with foreign-language support.
- **Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO) hotline app integration**: Found inside the Japan Official Travel App, it includes a one-tap connection to a multilingual call center (050-3816-2787) that operates 24/7, 365 days a year.
- **Google Translate's Conversation Mode**: Not a dedicated emergency app, but download the offline Japanese language pack now, before you need it, so it works even without signal during a disaster when networks are congested.

| Situation | Who to Contact | Number/App | Language Support |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fire or medical emergency | Fire/Ambulance | 119 | Japanese (request English via Japan Helpline relay) |
| Crime or immediate danger | Police | 110 | Japanese (same relay option) |
| General emergency translation help | Japan Helpline | 0570-000-911 | Multilingual, 24/7 |
| Non-urgent medical questions | AMDA Center | Varies by language line | Multilingual |
| Tourist/student general emergency | JNTO Hotline | 050-3816-2787 | Multilingual, 24/7 |

Save all five of these numbers directly into your phone's contacts labeled clearly in English **before** you need them — during an actual emergency is the worst time to be searching your call history.

## Building Your Phone's "Disaster Mode" Setup

Beyond installing apps, a few device-level settings dramatically improve your readiness:

- **Enable Emergency Alerts/Wireless Emergency Alerts** in your phone's native settings (iOS: Settings > Notifications > Government Alerts; Android: Settings > Safety & Emergency). These piggyback on Japan's carrier-level J-Alert system and work even without any app installed, often faster than third-party apps.
- **Download offline maps** of your neighborhood and commute route through Google Maps, since cellular networks frequently become overloaded within minutes of a major quake.
- **Set up an ICE (In Case of Emergency) contact** in your phone's medical ID feature, accessible from the lock screen even if the phone is passcode-locked, so first responders can identify you and notify someone.
- **Keep your phone above 50% charge** overnight when typhoon warnings are active, and know where your portable battery pack is. Power outages are more common during typhoons than during earthquakes.
- **Register with your university's international student safety network**, since most universities (and some language schools) run their own SMS or LINE-based emergency check-in system separate from government apps.

## What to Actually Do When an Alert Goes Off

Having the apps installed is only half the preparation. Muscle memory for the actual moment matters just as much.

**When an earthquake early warning sounds:**
1. Immediately get low — drop to the ground if you're able.
2. Move away from windows, mirrors, and tall furniture.
3. If near a doorway, brace yourself under something sturdy rather than standing in an open doorway (a common myth; modern Japanese buildings don't need doorway bracing, and debris risk is higher near doors).
4. Do not run outside during the shaking itself — falling tiles and glass are a major injury cause.
5. After shaking stops, check for gas leaks before using electrical switches, and follow local evacuation signage to your designated evacuation site (避難場所), which is usually posted at your apartment entrance or listed on your city ward's website.

**When a typhoon warning escalates to Level 4:**
1. Head to your city's designated evacuation shelter (避難所) — these are different from the earthquake "safe gathering" points, so confirm the typhoon-specific location in advance via your ward office's website.
2. Secure or bring indoors anything on balconies (bicycles, drying racks, plant pots) well before winds pick up.
3. Fill a bathtub with water if you expect an extended outage, since water pressure can drop during storms.
4. Avoid underpasses and riverside paths — flash flooding in urban underpasses causes more deaths annually than wind damage.

## Staying Prepared Beyond the Apps

Apps are your early-warning layer, but pair them with a physical go-bag containing a flashlight, a portable charger, copies of your residence card and passport, and a few days of water and non-perishable food. Most convenience stores (Lawson, FamilyMart, 7-Eleven) also serve as designated disaster relief stations stocked with emergency supplies — look for the sticker in the window. Combining digital alerts with this basic physical readiness means that when the notification sound you tested back on day one finally goes off for real, you'll already know exactly what to do next.
