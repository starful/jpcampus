## Akihabara: Your One-Stop Destination for PC Parts

If you're an international student in Tokyo looking to build a custom PC, Akihabara ("Akiba") is the answer to nearly every question you have about where to shop. This Tokyo neighborhood has been Japan's electronics epicenter for decades, and while it's now equally famous for anime and gaming culture, the multi-story component shops that line Chuo-dori and the surrounding backstreets remain some of the best places in the world to build a machine from scratch. Whether you need a flagship GPU, a budget CPU, or a single obscure cable, Akihabara has a shop that specializes in exactly that. This guide walks you through which stores to visit for which parts, how to use your tax-free status as a temporary visitor, and where to find used components if you're building on a student budget.

## Navigating the District: Where the Shops Cluster

Akihabara Station (JR Yamanote/Chuo-Sobu lines, and Hibiya/Tsukuba Express) drops you right in the middle of the action. Most PC-focused stores are within a 10-minute walk of the Electric Town Exit.

- **Chuo-dori (Main Street):** The big-name multi-floor retailers — Tsukumo, Sofmap, Yodobashi — are concentrated here.
- **Backstreets east of Chuo-dori (toward Kanda Myojin):** This is where you'll find specialist shops like Ark, Faith, and Tsukumo's separate CPU/parts buildings, along with smaller independent used-parts dealers.
- **Sotokanda area:** A cluster of small used-hardware and retro shops, great for hunting down deals or oddball components.

A realistic shopping day involves crisscrossing between these zones on foot, since many "single shops" are actually split across two or three nearby buildings by category (CPU building, GPU building, peripherals building).

## Best Shops by Component Type

Different stores in Akihabara have different specialties. Here's a breakdown of where locals and expats actually go for each part category.

| Component | Recommended Shop | Why Go Here |
|---|---|---|
| CPU | Tsukumo (CPU/Parts Bldg) | Deep stock of both Intel and AMD, staff post daily price comparisons on whiteboards |
| GPU | Tsukumo eX./Dospara | Dedicated GPU floors with open-box demo units running benchmarks |
| Motherboards | Dospara (Akihabara Main) | Huge selection across chipsets, good return policy for DOA boards |
| PSU/Case/Cooling | Ark (Bessokan) | Known for detailed spec sheets in English-friendly displays |
| Peripherals (keyboards, mice) | Yodobashi Akiba | Try-before-you-buy demo stations, largest general selection |
| Used/Budget Parts | Sofmap Used Parts Corner, Ark Used Section | Warrantied used GPUs and CPUs at 20-40% off retail |
| Cables, Adapters, Small Parts | Sengoku Densho, Aitendo | Bulk bins, extremely cheap, great for cable management projects |

### Tsukumo (ツクモ)

Tsukumo is one of the most trusted names in Akihabara and has been a go-to for serious builders for years. It operates several buildings near the station — one focused on CPUs and motherboards, another (Tsukumo eX.) dedicated almost entirely to graphics cards. Staff regularly update handwritten price boards comparing their prices to competitors, which is a great way to gauge whether you're getting a fair deal on any given day. Tsukumo is also known for running frequent bundle deals (CPU + motherboard combos), which can save first-time builders real money.

### Dospara (ドスパラ)

Dospara is a national chain, but its Akihabara flagship store is enormous and carries everything from entry-level parts to enthusiast-grade components. It's particularly strong for motherboards and pre-built systems, and the staff are used to dealing with a high volume of walk-in customers, so service is efficient. Dospara also has a solid trade-in and buyback program if you're upgrading later and want to sell your old parts.

### Ark (アーク)

Ark is beloved by more experienced builders because of its no-nonsense, detailed approach — component listings often include voltage specs, benchmark notes, and compatibility warnings that other shops skip. Ark's main store and its "Bessokan" (annex) a short walk away split inventory between mainstream parts and more niche items like server-grade hardware, workstation GPUs, and specialty cooling solutions.

## Making the Most of Tax-Free Shopping

One of the biggest financial perks available to international students and short-term visitors is Japan's **tax-free (免税) shopping system**, which lets eligible shoppers skip the 10% consumption tax on qualifying purchases.

**Who qualifies:**
- Tourists and short-term visitors holding a "Temporary Visitor" (短期滞在) stamp or sticker in their passport.
- **Important:** Most international students on a long-term student visa (留学) do **not** qualify for tax-free shopping, since the exemption is specifically for temporary visitors staying under 6 months. If you're studying in Japan long-term, you generally will not be eligible — but it's worth knowing for when friends or family visit and want to shop for you (they'll need their own passport).

**How it works when you are eligible:**
1. Spend a minimum of ¥5,000 (before tax) at a single store on the same day to qualify.
2. Bring your passport with you — stores cannot process tax-free purchases without scanning or checking it.
3. At checkout, tell the staff "免税でお願いします" (menzei de onegaishimasu — "tax-free, please") or simply say "tax-free."
4. Larger stores like Yodobashi, Dospara, and Sofmap have dedicated tax-free counters, often with English-speaking staff or English signage, where the paperwork is processed after your purchase.
5. A tax-free record will be electronically attached to your passport; you don't need to keep paper receipts for customs anymore under the current system, but keep your purchase confirmation just in case.
6. General items (electronics, PC parts) purchased tax-free must, in principle, be taken out of Japan — they aren't meant for consumption or resale domestically.

If you do qualify (or are shopping on someone else's temporary visitor status), prioritize your big-ticket purchases — GPU, CPU, monitor — at a single large store to hit the minimum threshold and maximize savings in one transaction.

## Hunting for Deals: The Used Parts Scene

For students watching every yen, Akihabara's secondhand component market is a goldmine that's often overlooked by newcomers who only visit the flashy main floors.

- **Sofmap** operates dedicated used-hardware sections (look for 中古 signage, meaning "used/secondhand") separate from its new-goods floors, often in a different part of the same building or a nearby annex.
- **Ark's used corner** is well organized by category and usually includes a grading system (A/B/C condition) with visible cosmetic notes and functional guarantees.
- **Sotokanda's smaller independent shops** are hit-or-miss but can have genuinely rare finds — older workstation cards, discontinued CPU sockets, and parts for retro builds — at prices well below what you'd find online.

**Tips for buying used safely:**
- Ask for the shop's warranty period (many offer 1-3 month warranties even on used items — always confirm before buying).
- Test GPUs in-store when possible; most used-parts counters have a bench setup for this.
- Bring cash — some smaller used shops and stalls don't accept foreign-issued credit cards.
- Avoid used PSUs unless the shop explicitly warranties them; power supplies are the one component where saving money isn't worth the risk to your other parts.

## A Practical One-Day Shopping Route

If you're trying to build an entire PC in a single trip, here's a sensible walking order that minimizes backtracking:

| Stop Order | Shop | What to Buy | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Tsukumo (CPU/Parts Bldg) | CPU, motherboard | Ask about bundle discounts |
| 2 | Tsukumo eX. | GPU | Compare against posted price board |
| 3 | Ark / Ark Bessokan | PSU, case, cooling | Check detailed spec sheets |
| 4 | Sofmap Used Corner | RAM, storage (if budget-conscious) | Confirm warranty terms |
| 5 | Dospara | Fill any remaining gaps | Good for last-minute swaps |
| 6 | Yodobashi Akiba | Peripherals, monitor, cables | Try demo units before buying |
| 7 | Sengoku Densho | Small parts, cable ties, adapters | Cheap, cash-friendly |

Plan for a full day if you're comparison-shopping seriously — prices can vary by ¥1,000-3,000 between stores for the same part, so a bit of legwork genuinely pays off.

## Practical Shopping Tips for Students

- **Bring cash as backup.** While major stores accept credit cards and IC cards (Suica/Pasmo), smaller shops and used-parts counters sometimes prefer or require cash.
- **Check compatibility before you buy.** Staff are generally helpful, but language barriers can make detailed compatibility questions tricky. Bring a written parts list (a spreadsheet on your phone works fine) so staff can quickly cross-reference sockets, wattage, and dimensions.
- **Ask about assembly support.** Some shops, including Dospara, offer paid PC assembly services if you'd rather not build solo — useful if this is your first build.
- **Keep your receipts.** Beyond tax-free purposes, Japanese electronics retailers are strict about requiring original receipts for any warranty claims or returns, typically within 7-14 days of purchase.
- **Visit on a weekday if possible.** Weekends bring heavy crowds, especially around Tsukumo and Dospara, making it harder to get hands-on time with demo units or staff attention.

Building a PC in Akihabara is as much an experience as it is a shopping trip — take your time, compare prices across at least two or three shops, and don't be afraid to ask questions even with limited Japanese. The staff at these stores build and troubleshoot PCs every single day, and most are happy to help you put together a machine you'll be proud of.
