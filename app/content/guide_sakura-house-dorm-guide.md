## Understanding Sakura House Costs: What You Actually Pay Before Moving In

If you're searching for a straightforward answer to "how much does Sakura House actually cost," here it is: unlike traditional Japanese apartments, Sakura House eliminates most of the notorious upfront costs — no key money (reikin), no agent fees, and no guarantor requirement. This is precisely why it's become one of the most popular housing options for international students and newcomers to Japan.

Here's a realistic breakdown of what you'll pay before you get your keys:

| Cost Item | Typical Amount | Required? |
|---|---|---|
| Deposit | 1 month's rent (refundable, minus cleaning) | Yes |
| Key money (reikin) | ¥0 | Not charged |
| Agency fee | ¥0 | Not charged |
| Administration fee | ¥5,000–¥15,000 (one-time) | Yes, varies by property |
| First month's rent | Full or prorated, depending on move-in date | Yes |
| Guarantor company fee | ¥0 | Not required |
| Key/card deposit | ¥5,000–¥10,000 (refundable) | Yes, at most properties |

For comparison, a standard Japanese rental apartment often demands key money equal to 1–2 months' rent, an agency fee equal to 1 month's rent, a guarantor or guarantor-company fee, and sometimes "renewal fees" (koushinryo) every one or two years. Sakura House strips nearly all of that away, which means your realistic move-in cost is roughly **2 months' rent plus a small administration fee** — compared to 4–6 months' rent upfront at a conventional apartment.

Rent itself varies significantly by room type and location:

- **Shared dorm rooms**: approximately ¥50,000–¥70,000/month
- **Private single rooms in a share house**: approximately ¥70,000–¥100,000/month
- **Studio apartments (1K/1R)**: approximately ¥90,000–¥150,000/month, depending on ward

Central Tokyo locations (Shinjuku, Shibuya, Ikebukuro) sit at the higher end of these ranges, while properties in Adachi, Itabashi, or further-out wards tend to run 10–20% cheaper.

## Monthly Fees and What's Genuinely Included in Your Rent

One of the biggest advantages of Sakura House — and the reason many students choose it over a standalone apartment — is that the advertised rent is close to your actual total monthly cost. There's no separate utility setup, no need to visit a Japanese utility company website, and no surprise bills in your mailbox.

Typically included in your monthly rent:

- Electricity, water, and gas
- High-speed internet (Wi-Fi) in your room or common areas
- Basic furniture: bed, mattress, desk, chair, closet/wardrobe
- Shared kitchen appliances (fridge, microwave, rice cooker, stove)
- Cleaning of common areas (kitchen, bathroom, lounge)
- 24-hour English and Japanese-speaking support staff
- Access to laundry machines (sometimes coin-operated, sometimes free)

Not typically included:

- Coin laundry usage fees (usually ¥200–¥400 per wash)
- Personal toiletries and bedding linens (sheets/towels can often be rented separately for a small fee)
- Renter's insurance, if required by the specific property (usually optional but recommended)
- Optional room cleaning service upgrades

Because utilities are bundled, your monthly spend is highly predictable — a real benefit for students managing a fixed study-abroad budget and relying on remittances or scholarship disbursements.

## Move-In Day: What Actually Happens When You Check In

Move-in day with Sakura House is designed to be far less bureaucratic than renting independently, but it still follows a clear procedure. Knowing it in advance will save you a confusing first day.

**Before you arrive**, you'll need to have completed:

1. Online application and room reservation
2. Payment of deposit + first month's rent (bank transfer or credit card, depending on the branch)
3. Submission of required documents (see table below)
4. Confirmation email with your move-in date, time window, and office address

**Documents to bring on move-in day:**

| Document | Notes |
|---|---|
| Passport | Original required, not a copy |
| Residence card (Zairyu Card) | If you already have one; otherwise provide it within the grace period |
| Printed confirmation email/reservation | Speeds up front-desk processing |
| Proof of payment | Screenshot or receipt of deposit/rent transfer |
| Emergency contact information | Name and phone number, Japan-based if possible |
| Passport-sized photo (occasionally requested) | Used for internal resident ID at some branches |

**On the day itself**, the general flow looks like this:

1. Arrive at the designated Sakura House office (not always the building itself — some check-ins happen at a central office first).
2. Staff verify your identity and documents, and confirm your payment.
3. You sign the rental agreement/house rules acknowledgment.
4. You receive your key or key card, plus a short orientation covering trash sorting rules, quiet hours, and common area etiquette.
5. Staff walk you to your room (or give directions) and do a quick room-condition check together — take photos of any existing damage or wear at this stage.
6. You get a welcome guide with Wi-Fi details, laundry instructions, and emergency contacts.

A tip from past residents: **take dated photos of your room's condition during this walkthrough.** It protects your deposit refund later, since any pre-existing scuffs or marks are already documented.

## Sakura House Compared to Other Dorm and Housing Options

Sakura House isn't the only option for international students, and it's worth knowing where it sits relative to university dormitories, UR apartments, and private share houses.

| Option | Upfront Cost | Monthly Cost | Guarantor Needed | English Support | Flexibility |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sakura House | ~2 months' rent | ¥50,000–¥150,000 | No | Yes, dedicated | High (short-term OK) |
| University dormitory | Low (often 1 month + small fee) | ¥20,000–¥50,000 | Usually no | Varies by school | Low (academic-year bound) |
| Private apartment (independent) | 4–6 months' rent | Rent + separate utilities | Yes, usually | Rare | Medium (1–2 year leases) |
| UR Rental Housing | 2–3 months' rent, no key money | Rent + separate utilities | No | Limited | Medium |
| Other share houses (e.g., Oak House, Social Residence) | ~1–2 months' rent | ¥50,000–¥90,000 | No | Varies | High |

University dormitories are usually the cheapest option, but they're often restricted to first-year exchange students, have strict curfews, and come with limited availability — many schools have long waitlists. UR housing offers no key money but requires you to independently set up electricity, gas, and internet, which is a real hurdle if your Japanese is still developing. Independent apartments offer the most privacy and long-term value but demand the highest upfront cost and usually a guarantor, which international students without a long Japan credit history often struggle to secure.

Sakura House occupies a practical middle ground: higher monthly cost than a dorm, but far lower upfront barrier than an independent lease, with built-in support in English — which is the deciding factor for many first-time arrivals.

## Why Sakura House Remains a Favorite Among International Students

Beyond the cost structure, a few specific features explain Sakura House's popularity:

- **No guarantor, no Japanese co-signer.** This alone removes the single biggest obstacle international students face when apartment hunting in Japan.
- **Short-term contracts available.** Many properties allow stays as short as one month, which suits exchange students, intensive language program students, or anyone unsure of their long-term plans.
- **English-language contracts and support staff.** You can ask questions, report maintenance issues, or resolve disputes without needing advanced Japanese.
- **Furnished rooms from day one.** You arrive with a suitcase, not a shopping list for a bed frame and kitchen table.
- **Community living.** Shared kitchens and lounges in many properties create a built-in social network — useful when you're new to the country and don't yet have a friend group.
- **Wide property network across Tokyo** (and some other cities), so you can often switch locations within the Sakura House system if your university or job location changes.

## Practical Tips From Residents Who've Been Through It

- **Book 3–4 weeks ahead of your target move-in date**, especially for popular wards like Shinjuku or Shibuya during the April and September intake seasons when student demand spikes.
- **Ask specifically which utilities are capped.** Some properties have a fair-use electricity limit before extra charges apply — confirm this during inquiry, not after your first bill.
- **Read the house rules on trash sorting carefully.** Japan's garbage separation rules are strict, and shared housing enforces them seriously; getting it wrong repeatedly can lead to warnings.
- **Clarify the deposit refund timeline before signing.** Most refunds are processed within a few weeks after move-out, minus any documented cleaning or damage charges.
- **Visit the common kitchen and bathroom during a viewing if possible**, or ask for recent photos — shared facility quality varies noticeably between older and newer properties.
- **Keep your rental agreement and payment receipts digitally and on paper.** You'll need these for visa-related address proof, bank account opening, and phone contracts.

With the cost structure demystified and the move-in process mapped out, Sakura House offers a genuinely low-friction entry point into Japanese housing — ideal for students who want to focus their energy on settling into university life, not navigating a maze of guarantor paperwork and key money negotiations.
