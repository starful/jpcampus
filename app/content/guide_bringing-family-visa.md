## Can Students Bring Their Spouse or Children to Japan?

Yes — international students on a "Student" (留学) visa in Japan can bring their spouse and children through a "Dependent" (家族滞在, *Kazoku Taizai*) visa. This isn't automatic, though. You'll need to prove you can financially support your family, complete separate application paperwork for each dependent, and understand that your spouse or children will face their own work restrictions once they arrive. This guide walks through exactly what's required, how much money you actually need, and what life looks like for a dependent family member living in Japan.

The Dependent visa is specifically designed for the legal spouse or unmarried minor children of a visa holder in a qualifying status — student visas included. Note that parents, siblings, and adult children generally do not qualify under this category, no matter how dependent they may be on you financially.

## Who Qualifies as a Dependent

Not every family member is eligible. Immigration authorities apply a narrow definition:

- **Legal spouse** — a legally married husband or wife. Common-law or de facto partners do not qualify, and same-sex marriages are only recognized if legally performed and the marriage is recognized under Japanese administrative practice (this varies by case and is worth confirming with an immigration lawyer or the local immigration bureau).
- **Children** — biological or legally adopted children who are unmarried and typically minors. Adult children (generally 20+) are usually excluded, even if they are still financially dependent on you.
- **Excluded relatives** — parents, parents-in-law, siblings, and fiancé(e)s cannot be sponsored under this visa category, regardless of how much support they need.

If you have a family member who doesn't fit this narrow definition (such as an aging parent), they would need to explore a separate visa category — this is worth flagging early since many students assume the Dependent visa covers all close family.

## Financial Requirements: How Much Do You Need to Show?

This is where many student applicants get tripped up. Japan does not publish one fixed minimum income figure for Dependent visa sponsorship, but immigration officers evaluate whether your combined income and savings are sufficient to support your family without relying on illegal work or public assistance.

In practice, here's what officers typically look for:

| Factor | What's Evaluated |
|---|---|
| Scholarship or stipend amount | Monthly/annual scholarship income, if any |
| Personal or family savings | Bank statements showing liquid funds (often 6–12 months' worth of living costs) |
| Sponsor's income (parents/relatives) | Remittance history and a formal letter of guarantee if family abroad is supporting you |
| Housing costs | Whether rent is realistic for the number of dependents |
| Number of dependents | More dependents = higher expected support amount |

As a rough planning benchmark, many students find they need to demonstrate combined resources equivalent to at least **¥2,000,000–¥3,000,000 per year** for a spouse, and additional amounts per child — though this is not an official government figure, and requirements vary by immigration bureau and case officer. Tokyo and Osaka bureaus, which see far higher application volumes, tend to scrutinize weaker financial documentation more closely than smaller regional offices.

### Documents You'll Typically Need to Submit

| Document | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Certificate of Enrollment | Confirms your current student status |
| Bank statements (last 3–6 months) | Proves available funds |
| Scholarship award letter (if applicable) | Shows recurring stipend income |
| Letter of guarantee (身元保証書) | Formal commitment from you as sponsor, sometimes co-signed by a parent |
| Remittance receipts | Evidence that money is regularly sent from home, if relevant |
| Marriage certificate / birth certificate (with certified Japanese translation) | Proves the legal family relationship |
| Certificate of Eligibility (COE) application form | Filed on behalf of each dependent |

A weak application is usually one where the numbers look thin relative to the cost of living in your city — Tokyo, Osaka, and Kyoto living costs run noticeably higher than smaller cities like Fukuoka, Sendai, or Sapporo, and immigration officers factor this into their assessment even though it's not written into any formal checklist.

## Applying for the Certificate of Eligibility (COE)

Before your spouse or child can apply for a visa at a Japanese embassy or consulate abroad, you (or a representative in Japan, such as your school's international office or an administrative scrivener) need to apply for a **Certificate of Eligibility (COE)** on their behalf at the Regional Immigration Services Bureau in Japan.

Typical steps:

1. **Gather documentation** — enrollment certificate, financial proof, and the family relationship documents listed above.
2. **Submit the COE application** — filed at the immigration bureau covering your address in Japan (this can often be done by a proxy, such as your university's international student office, saving you a trip).
3. **Wait for processing** — typically **1 to 3 months**, though timing fluctuates with application volume and how straightforward your documentation is.
4. **Receive the COE** — once approved, it's mailed or handed to you in Japan; you then send the original to your family member abroad.
5. **Visa application abroad** — your spouse or child submits the COE, passport, and application form at the nearest Japanese embassy or consulate in their home country.
6. **Visa issuance** — typically takes about **5 business days** after submission, though this varies by country and consulate workload.

Plan for the whole process, start to finish, to take **2 to 4 months**. If you're aiming for your family to arrive at the start of a semester, start the COE application at least 4 months in advance.

## Work Restrictions for Dependents

This is one of the most misunderstood parts of the Dependent visa, and it's critical to get right — working outside these limits can jeopardize both the dependent's status and your own student visa.

- A Dependent visa **does not include work authorization by default**. Your spouse or child cannot legally work upon arrival.
- To work, the dependent must separately apply for **permission to engage in activity other than that permitted by the status of residence previously granted** (資格外活動許可), submitted at the immigration bureau or, in some cases, at the airport upon arrival.
- Once granted, this permission caps work at **28 hours per week** — the same limit that applies to the student visa holder themselves.
- Dependents **cannot work in businesses affecting public morals** — this includes bars, nightclubs, adult entertainment venues, and pachinko parlors, among others.
- There is **no equivalent to the "long vacation" exception** that student visa holders get (where students can work close to full-time during school breaks). The 28-hour cap applies to dependents year-round, with no seasonal increase.

| Visa Holder | Default Work Rights | Max Weekly Hours (with permission) | Vacation Exception |
|---|---|---|---|
| Student (you) | None by default; needs permit | 28 hrs/week | Up to ~40 hrs/week during long school breaks |
| Dependent (spouse/child) | None by default; needs permit | 28 hrs/week | None — flat 28 hrs/week year-round |

Because of this cap, most families relying on a Dependent visa should not expect the accompanying spouse to meaningfully offset household costs through part-time work — it can help, but 28 hours a week at typical part-time wages won't cover a family's full cost of living in most Japanese cities.

## Health Insurance, School Enrollment, and Daily Life Logistics

Once your family arrives, a few practical matters need quick attention:

- **National Health Insurance (国民健康保険)** — dependents residing in Japan for more than 3 months are required to enroll, typically at the local municipal office within 14 days of receiving their Residence Card. Premiums are based on household income and city of residence.
- **Residence Card and municipal registration** — dependents receive a Residence Card at the port of entry (for most nationalities) and must register their address at the local municipal office within 14 days of moving in.
- **School enrollment for children** — Japanese public elementary and junior high schools are generally open to foreign children and are tuition-free, though enrollment procedures and available language support vary significantly by municipality. Larger cities like Tokyo, Yokohama, and Osaka tend to have more established support systems (translators, Japanese-language classes) than smaller towns.
- **Visa renewal alignment** — the Dependent visa's period of stay is generally tied to the primary student visa holder's status, so renewals should be planned together, and any lapse in your own student status directly jeopardizes your dependents' legal residence.

## Weighing the Decision: Is Bringing Family the Right Call?

Before committing to the process, it's worth being honest about the tradeoffs:

- **Financial reality** — tuition, your own living costs, and now a family's living costs (with limited dependent work income) add up quickly, especially in Tokyo or Osaka.
- **Emotional and logistical support** — many students find that having family present significantly eases the stress of studying abroad, particularly for longer degree programs (2+ years).
- **Timeline pressure** — if your program is short (a 1-year exchange or short certificate course), the 2–4 month COE process may eat up a disproportionate share of your time together.
- **Administrative burden** — you'll be the one managing paperwork, translations, and renewals for your entire family, on top of your coursework.

There's no universally right answer here — it depends heavily on your program length, financial cushion, and personal circumstances. But going in with realistic expectations about costs, timelines, and work limitations will save you from unpleasant surprises after your family has already committed to the move.
