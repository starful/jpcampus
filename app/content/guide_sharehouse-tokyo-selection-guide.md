## Share House vs. Apartment vs. Dorm: Which Fits Your Life in Tokyo?

Before you start scrolling listings, settle the bigger question: is a share house actually the right housing type for you? Each option trades cost, flexibility, and social life differently, and the "best" choice depends on your budget, how long you're staying, and whether you want a built-in social network from day one.

| Feature | Share House | Private Apartment | University Dorm |
|---|---|---|---|
| Key money / deposit | Often ¥0–30,000 | Usually 2–4 months' rent | ¥0–50,000 |
| Guarantor required | Rarely | Almost always | No |
| Minimum contract | 1 month | 1–2 years | 1 semester |
| Furnished | Yes | Rarely | Yes |
| Shared facilities | Kitchen, bath, lounge | None | Varies |
| Social community | High | None | Moderate |
| Privacy | Low–Medium | High | Low |
| Typical monthly cost (Tokyo) | ¥50,000–85,000 | ¥80,000–150,000 | ¥30,000–60,000 |

Share houses sit in a sweet spot for international students: far less paperwork than renting a private apartment (no guarantor, no key money negotiations, often no Japanese-language contract review), but more independence and a wider choice of neighborhoods than a university dorm. If you're staying less than a year, want to meet people quickly, or don't yet have a Japanese bank account or phone number, a share house is usually the most realistic entry point into Tokyo housing.

## Breaking Down the Real Cost of a Tokyo Share House

Share house marketing often advertises a low headline rent, but the total monthly outlay is what matters for your budget. Here's what to actually expect, based on typical listings from operators like Oakhouse, Sakura House, and Tokyo-based independent houses:

| Cost Item | Typical Range (Monthly) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Base rent | ¥45,000–75,000 | Varies heavily by ward and room type (private vs. shared room) |
| Utilities (electricity, water, gas) | ¥8,000–15,000 | Sometimes bundled into a flat "utility fee," sometimes metered separately |
| Internet | ¥0–3,000 | Most share houses include Wi-Fi free; confirm speed if you'll be on video calls |
| Common area/cleaning fee | ¥2,000–5,000 | Covers shared kitchen/bathroom cleaning and supplies |
| Key money (reikin) | ¥0 | Almost never charged at share houses—this is one of their biggest advantages |
| Deposit (shikikin) | ¥0–30,000 | Usually refundable minus cleaning costs; always get this in writing |
| Initial admin/registration fee | ¥10,000–20,000 | One-time, non-refundable |
| Guarantor company fee | ¥0 | Not required for almost all share houses |

A realistic all-in monthly budget for a decent share house room in Tokyo is **¥55,000–90,000**, with cheaper shared-room options (bunk-style or multi-person rooms) dropping as low as ¥40,000 in areas further from the center. Compare that to a standard private apartment, where you'd need ¥300,000–500,000 upfront just to move in (deposit, key money, agency fee, insurance, lock-change fee) before paying a single month's rent.

**Questions to ask before signing anything:**

- Is electricity/gas billed by usage or included flat? Ask for a sample bill from a current resident if possible.
- Is the deposit refundable in full if I leave the room clean, or is there an automatic cleaning deduction?
- Does the monthly fee change if I stay longer than 6 or 12 months? Many houses offer long-term discounts.
- Are bedding, a desk, and storage included, or will I need to buy them separately?

## Tokyo Neighborhoods Where International Students Actually Live

Location affects not just your commute but your weekly spending on transport, your social life, and how easily you can find affordable food. These are the areas where share houses cluster around student and young-professional populations:

**Takadanobaba & Waseda** — Home to Waseda University and several Japanese language schools, this area has the highest density of student-oriented share houses in Tokyo. Cheap ramen, used bookstores, and a genuinely international crowd. Rent tends to run slightly below the city average.

**Ikebukuro** — A major transit hub (JR Yamanote, Seibu, Tobu lines) with a large Chinese and broader international community. Excellent for grocery access (international supermarkets) and nightlife, but can feel chaotic for students who prefer quieter streets.

**Koenji & Nakano** — Popular with students who want a more "local Tokyo" vibe: vintage shops, live music venues, and lower rents than central wards, while still being 10–15 minutes from Shinjuku by train.

**Shin-Okubo** — Tokyo's Koreatown, directly on the Yamanote Line. Great food scene and convenience, with share houses priced similarly to Takadanobaba since it's one stop away.

**Kichijoji** — Slightly pricier but consistently ranked among Tokyo's most livable neighborhoods; good for students who want green space (Inokashira Park) and a calmer pace while still being 15 minutes from Shinjuku.

**Oimachi / Shinagawa side** — Favored by students attending universities in the southern part of the city or doing internships near business districts; less "studenty" in atmosphere but very convenient for Haneda Airport access.

## Reading a Share House Listing Like a Local

Listings on sites like Oakhouse, Sakura House, Hitsuji Real Estate, or GaijinPot Housing follow a predictable format once you know what to check. Go through this in order:

1. **Room type** — "private room," "semi-private" (a partitioned section of a larger room), or "shared/dorm room" (2–4 people). Semi-private rooms save money but offer little real privacy; confirm partition height and whether there's a lockable door.
2. **Floor plan and size** — Tokyo share house private rooms commonly run 6–9 m². Anything advertised without a stated square meterage is worth asking about directly.
3. **Bathroom ratio** — Check how many residents share each toilet and shower. A ratio worse than 1 bathroom per 4–5 people usually means morning queues.
4. **Kitchen equipment** — Confirm there's a working stove (not just a single IH burner), a reasonably sized fridge, and enough storage so your food doesn't get mixed up with others'.
5. **House rules document** — Ask to see this before committing. It should cover quiet hours, guest policy, cleaning rotation, and move-out notice period (commonly 1 month).
6. **Photos vs. reality** — Request recent photos of the specific room, not just stock photos of the "house type." Rooms within the same house can vary significantly in condition.
7. **Cancellation policy** — Know exactly how much notice you must give and whether you forfeit any deposit for early termination, especially important if your visa or program timeline is uncertain.

## Roommate Compatibility: What to Ask Before You Move In

Share house satisfaction depends less on the building and more on who else lives there. Most reputable share houses will let you visit before signing, or at minimum provide a resident profile breakdown (nationalities, age range, occupation mix). Use the visit or inquiry to ask:

- What's the current nationality and language mix? A house that's 90% one nationality can be great for comfort or isolating if you're hoping to practice Japanese or meet a mixed group.
- What's the average age and occupation (students vs. working professionals)? Professional-heavy houses are often quieter on weeknights.
- How often does the operator organize house events? Some share house companies run regular welcome parties, trips, or seasonal events that make integration much easier for new arrivals.
- What's the turnover rate? High turnover (people leaving within 1–2 months) can signal underlying problems with the house.
- Is there a house manager on-site or nearby, and how are conflicts between residents actually resolved?

If you can, ask to speak briefly with a current resident, independent of the agent showing you around. Honest feedback about noise, cleanliness habits, and the general atmosphere is far more useful than the official pitch.

## Red Flags That Signal a Share House to Avoid

Watch for these warning signs during your search, whether you're browsing online or touring in person:

- **No written house rules** — if staff can't produce a clear document covering quiet hours, cleaning duties, and guest policy, expect ongoing disputes.
- **Vague or shifting utility fees** — operators who can't give you last month's actual utility bill from a similar room, or who change the quoted "flat fee" between your inquiry and contract, are not being transparent.
- **Deposit described as "non-refundable regardless of condition"** — a legitimate share house deposit is refundable minus actual cleaning/damage costs, not an automatic forfeit.
- **No visible fire safety equipment or blocked emergency exits** — a basic but important check during any in-person tour.
- **Overcrowded kitchens/bathrooms relative to resident count** — if the listed resident capacity feels mismatched with the shared facilities during your tour, trust what you see over what's advertised.
- **Pressure to sign same-day without a cooling-off period** — reputable operators are comfortable with you taking 24–48 hours to decide.
- **Reviews mentioning unresponsive management** — search the operator's name plus "review" in English and Japanese; slow maintenance response is one of the most common share house complaints.

## Finishing the Move-In Process

Once you've chosen a house, the paperwork is lighter than renting privately, but still requires preparation:

- **Residence card (在留カード)** — mandatory for almost all applications; bring the physical card, not a photo.
- **Passport** — for identity verification alongside your residence card.
- **Proof of enrollment or employment** — a certificate of enrollment (在学証明書) from your school, or an offer/employment letter if you're working.
- **Proof of income or financial support** — some operators request a bank statement or scholarship letter, especially for stays longer than six months.
- **Emergency contact in Japan** — many houses ask for this even without a formal guarantor; your school's international office can often serve this role.

Most share houses can process an application within 2–5 business days and allow move-in as soon as the room is vacant, which is often within one to two weeks of signing—far faster than the 3–4 week turnaround typical for private apartments. Book a short-term option (weekly hotel or guesthouse) as a buffer if your ideal share house room isn't available the exact week you land, rather than rushing into a house that doesn't fit just to avoid a few extra nights of temporary accommodation.
