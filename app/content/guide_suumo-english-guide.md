## Understanding SUUMO's English Interface and What You'll Find There

SUUMO (suumo.jp) is Japan's largest property portal, but most listings are only in Japanese. For international students, the fastest way in is through **SUUMO's partner services and English-friendly portals** that pull from the same listing pools, such as GaijinPot Housing, Real Estate Japan, and Sakura House, or by using a browser translation extension directly on SUUMO itself. Chrome's built-in translate feature works surprisingly well on SUUMO's standardized listing pages, since the layout is consistent and translates cleanly field by field.

When you open a listing, you'll typically see this information laid out in a grid:

- **物件名 (Bukken-mei)** — Building name
- **賃料 (Chinryo)** — Monthly rent
- **管理費 (Kanrihi)** — Management/common fee
- **敷金/礼金 (Shikikin/Reikin)** — Deposit/key money
- **間取り (Madori)** — Floor plan (e.g., 1K, 1DK, 1LDK)
- **専有面積 (Senyuu Menseki)** — Floor area in square meters
- **築年数 (Chikunensuu)** — Building age

Knowing these labels by sight lets you scan listings quickly even without a full translation, and it helps when you're comparing screenshots sent by an agent who only responds in Japanese.

## Mansion, Apaato, and Ryo: Decoding Japanese Housing Vocabulary

One of the most confusing parts of apartment hunting in Japan is that "mansion" does not mean what English speakers expect. Getting these terms right will save you from filtering out buildings you actually want.

| Japanese Term | What It Actually Means | Typical Construction | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|
| マンション (Manshon) | A mid-to-high-rise apartment/condo building, usually concrete | Reinforced concrete (RC) | Students wanting better soundproofing and security |
| アパート (Apaato) | A low-rise apartment building, usually 2 stories | Wood or light steel frame | Budget-conscious students, often cheaper rent |
| 寮 (Ryo) | Dormitory, often university-affiliated or company housing | Varies | First-time students wanting community and simplicity |
| シェアハウス (Share House) | Shared house with private room, communal kitchen/bath | Varies | Students wanting lower costs and built-in social life |
| ワンルーム (One Room) | Studio apartment, no separated living space | Varies | Solo students prioritizing privacy and simplicity |

A quick rule of thumb: if soundproofing and insulation matter to you (especially in winter), lean toward manshon over apaato. The difference in construction material genuinely affects noise from neighbors and heating costs, and it's reflected in the rent.

## Using SUUMO's Filters Strategically

SUUMO's search filters are powerful, but they're easy to misuse if you don't understand how Japanese rental listings are structured. Here's how to set them up effectively:

- **Area and Line (エリア・沿線)**: Search by train line and station rather than by neighborhood name — this is how most Japanese renters think about location, and it will surface more results than searching a ward name alone.
- **Walking time to station (駅徒歩)**: Filter by minutes on foot (e.g., "within 10 minutes"). Note that this number is calculated at 80 meters per minute on flat ground, so actual walking time can feel longer with hills, stairs, or traffic lights.
- **Rent range (賃料)**: Set this *excluding* management fees, since SUUMO lists them separately. Always add the management fee (管理費/共益費) back in — it's often ¥3,000–¥15,000/month and is not optional.
- **Floor plan (間取り)**: 1K/1DK/1LDK are the most relevant for single students. The number indicates bedroom count; K = kitchen space, DK = dining-kitchen, LDK = living-dining-kitchen.
- **Building age (築年数)**: Older buildings (築20年+) are cheaper but may lack modern insulation or have shared equipment. Many are still perfectly livable — don't rule them out automatically.
- **Conditions (こだわり条件)**: This is where you filter for student-relevant amenities — look for 「学生可」(students accepted), 「外国人可」(foreign residents accepted), and 「保証人不要」(no guarantor required).

The "学生可" and "外国人可" filters are the two most important for international students, because a significant number of landlords restrict listings to Japanese nationals or working professionals only. Filtering these in from the start saves hours of contacting agents about properties you're not eligible for.

## What Landlords Require Before You Can Rent

Japanese landlords evaluate applicants more strictly than in many Western countries, and most require documentation beyond a simple application form. Before you start contacting agents, gather these in advance:

| Requirement | Details | Notes for International Students |
|---|---|---|
| Guarantor (保証人) | A person who agrees to cover unpaid rent | Many landlords now accept a guarantor company (保証会社) instead — ask upfront |
| Proof of income or enrollment | Scholarship letter, bank statement, or university enrollment certificate | University-issued enrollment certificates (在学証明書) often substitute for income proof |
| Residence Card (在留カード) | Valid visa status | Must show at least the remaining duration of your study period |
| Emergency contact in Japan | A local contact, sometimes a university staff member | Many universities provide an international office contact for this |
| Guarantor company fee | Typically ¥15,000–¥30,000 (one-time) or ~50% of one month's rent | Covers the risk the company takes on in place of a personal guarantor |

If you don't have a personal guarantor (most students don't), ask the agent directly: "保証会社は使えますか？" ("Can I use a guarantor company?"). The vast majority of agencies working with international tenants already have a standing relationship with one, so this is rarely a dealbreaker — it just adds a small fee.

## Real Estate Agents vs. Direct Landlord Contact

On SUUMO, you are almost always contacting a **real estate agency (不動産会社)**, not the landlord directly — even listings that look like they're from a property management company are usually relayed through an agent who holds the viewing keys and handles paperwork.

- **Going through an agent (most common path)**: You message or call the agency listed on the SUUMO page. They schedule viewings, translate conditions if bilingual, and process your application. Agency fee is typically **half a month's rent + consumption tax**, capped by law at one month's rent.
- **Share houses and some foreign-friendly operators**: Companies like Sakura House or Oakhouse manage their own properties directly, skip the agent fee entirely, and often allow move-in without a guarantor — a good option if you want to avoid the full traditional process.
- **University-affiliated housing**: Contact your school's international student office directly rather than SUUMO; many partner with specific agencies or operate their own dormitories with simplified paperwork.

When contacting an agent, send a short, clear message rather than a long email. A simple template works well:

> "Hello, I'm interested in [property name/listing URL]. I am an international student starting at [university] in [month]. Could you tell me if foreign students are accepted, and whether a guarantor company is available? Thank you."

Agents handling many foreign applicants respond faster to concise messages like this than to long explanations, since they can quickly check eligibility and reply.

## Initial Costs: Deposit, Key Money, and the Full Move-In Breakdown

This is the part that surprises almost every first-time renter in Japan: the money needed upfront is far more than just one month's rent. Budget for the following, typically due in full before you receive your keys:

| Cost Item | Japanese Term | Typical Amount | Refundable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deposit | 敷金 (Shikikin) | 1 month's rent | Partially, minus cleaning/repair deductions |
| Key money | 礼金 (Reikin) | 0–1 month's rent | No — this is a gift to the landlord, non-refundable |
| Agency fee | 仲介手数料 (Chuukai Tesuryo) | 0.5–1 month's rent + tax | No |
| Guarantor company fee | 保証会社利用料 | ¥15,000–30,000 or ~50% rent | No |
| Fire insurance | 火災保険 | ¥15,000–20,000/2 years | No |
| First month's rent | 前家賃 (Maeyachin) | 1 month's rent (sometimes prorated) | N/A |
| Lock change fee | 鍵交換費 | ¥15,000–25,000 | No |

In total, expect to pay **4–6 months' worth of rent** as a lump sum at move-in. For a ¥70,000/month apartment, this often means ¥280,000–¥420,000 upfront — budget for this well before your target move-in date, since agencies generally require full payment before handing over keys.

Some buildings advertise 「礼金ゼロ」(zero key money) or 「ゼロゼロ物件」(zero-zero properties, meaning no deposit and no key money) — these are worth prioritizing in your SUUMO search filters if your budget is tight, though they sometimes come with slightly higher monthly rent or a shorter minimum contract term to offset the lower entry cost.

## The Move-In Inspection and Signing the Contract

Once your application is approved, the process moves through a few final, important steps:

1. **Contract review (契約書確認)**: You'll receive the lease (賃貸借契約書) and an "important matters explanation" (重要事項説明) — by law, a licensed agent must walk you through this document, including rules on renewal fees, pet policies, and exit cleaning costs.
2. **Key handover (鍵の受け渡し)**: Usually happens on your move-in date, sometimes a day or two before if arranged with the agent.
3. **Move-in inspection (入居時チェック)**: Walk through the unit with a checklist, photographing any existing scratches, stains, or damage. This record protects your deposit refund later — Japanese landlords are strict about charging for "new" damage at move-out, so documenting the original condition is essential.
4. **Utility setup**: Electricity (電気) and water (水道) can usually be activated online or by phone same-day; gas (ガス) often requires an in-person technician visit, so schedule this at least a few days before move-in.

Keep copies of everything — the signed contract, your move-in inspection photos, and all payment receipts — in a dedicated folder. If a dispute arises over deposit deductions when you eventually move out, this documentation is your strongest protection.

With the vocabulary, filters, and cost structure above, you're well equipped to search SUUMO confidently, ask agents the right questions, and avoid the most common surprises international students face when renting in Japan for the first time.
