## Building an IT Career in Japan After Graduation

Japan's tech sector is short an estimated 450,000 to nearly 800,000 engineers by 2030, according to METI projections, and companies from Rakuten to Mercari to LINE Yahoo are actively opening headcount for global talent. That's the good news. The complicated news is that the traditional path into a Japanese company — the *shukatsu* cycle of company briefings, entry sheets, and group interviews starting a full year before graduation — was built for generalist hires, not engineers. If you're aiming for a software engineering, data, or infrastructure role, you don't need to run that gauntlet. There's a parallel track built around portfolios, technical assessments, and specialist recruiters, and it moves faster and rewards demonstrable skill over interview polish. This guide lays out that track step by step.

## Why the Standard Shukatsu Track Isn't Built for Engineers

Traditional new-graduate hiring (*shinsotsu saiyo*) in Japan is designed around a single hiring cohort that starts in April, with recruiting activity beginning as early as 14–16 months prior. It emphasizes:

- Uniform entry sheets (ES) answering standardized questions about your "gakuchika" (student life achievements)
- Group discussions and multiple rounds of interviews focused on communication style and cultural fit
- Hiring for a rotating generalist track, where new hires may be placed in sales, HR, or planning regardless of their degree

Engineering hiring at most tech-forward companies has split off from this into a separate track called *engineer saiyo* or *career/chuto saiyo*-style hiring even for new grads. This track:

- Runs year-round, not just once per year
- Prioritizes a portfolio, GitHub activity, and a coding test over entry sheet essays
- Often skips the group interview format entirely in favor of 1:1 technical interviews
- Is far more open to non-Japanese applicants and mid-level Japanese language ability, because the evaluation criteria are technical

If your Japanese is below business level (N2 or below) but your coding ability is strong, this engineer-specific track is almost always your better entry point. Companies like Rakuten, Mercari, SmartNews, and PayPay run English-medium engineering interview tracks specifically because they recruit globally.

## What Technical Skills Are Actually in Demand

Demand varies significantly by sector. Based on job postings from Japanese tech recruiters and platforms like Wantedly, Green, and BizReach in 2025–2026, here's how demand breaks down:

| Skill Area | Demand Level | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Backend (Java, Go, Kotlin) | Very High | Legacy enterprise systems (Java) plus modern fintech/SaaS (Go, Kotlin) |
| Cloud infrastructure (AWS, GCP) | Very High | AWS dominates; certified engineers are prioritized |
| Data engineering / analytics | High | Especially at retail (Rakuten), gaming (Cygames), and fintech |
| Frontend (React, TypeScript, Vue) | High | Strong at startups; less critical at SIer (system integrator) firms |
| Machine learning / AI engineering | High, rising fast | Concentrated in Tokyo; smaller applicant pool means less competition |
| Mobile (Swift, Kotlin) | Moderate | Consistent but narrower than web/backend roles |
| SAP / legacy enterprise systems | Moderate | Common at large SIer firms (NTT Data, Fujitsu), less exciting but stable visas |
| DevOps / SRE | High | Increasingly bundled with cloud roles |

Two dynamics matter here. First, Japan's IT industry has an unusually large "SIer" segment — large system integrators that build and maintain enterprise software for banks, government, and manufacturers. These firms hire in huge volume, sponsor visas reliably, but often use older tech stacks (Java, COBOL-adjacent legacy systems) and slower development cycles. Second, the startup and "mega-venture" segment (Mercari, SmartNews, LayerX, Money Forward) offers modern stacks and better pay-for-skill alignment but has smaller headcount and more competitive interviews.

If your goal is to break in quickly and build a resume, SIer firms are easier. If your goal is to maximize skill growth and compensation, mega-ventures and startups are the target, but expect to need a stronger portfolio.

## Building a Portfolio That Actually Gets You Interviews

Japanese engineering managers, like their counterparts everywhere, weight a working portfolio far more heavily than a GPA or a list of coursework. But there are some Japan-specific expectations worth knowing:

- **A public GitHub with real commit history matters more here than in some markets.** Recruiters and hiring managers routinely check contribution graphs before scheduling a first interview. Sparse, template-based repos are an instant red flag.
- **Build at least one full-stack project solo, and one collaborative project (even a small open-source contribution).** This demonstrates you can work in a team context, which Japanese employers weigh heavily given the collaborative nature of most dev teams.
- **Document your README in both English and, if possible, simple Japanese.** Even a partial Japanese README signals effort and language investment, which recruiters flag positively regardless of your actual JLPT level.
- **Deploy something live.** A project sitting only in a local repo is worth far less than the same project deployed on AWS, Vercel, or Render with a working URL you can share in an interview.

A strong new-grad portfolio for the Japanese market typically includes:

1. One deployed full-stack application (with auth, a database, and a real use case — not a to-do app clone)
2. Two to three smaller projects showing range (a CLI tool, a data pipeline, an API)
3. Visible contribution activity — commits spread across months, not a single upload week before job hunting
4. A pinned repo or personal site that functions as your portfolio homepage, linked directly in your resume and LinkedIn

## Passing Japanese Coding Tests and Technical Interviews

Coding assessments are now standard at nearly every tech-forward employer, including SIer firms hiring for cloud/DX (digital transformation) roles. Expect one or more of the following formats:

| Assessment Type | Common Platforms | What to Prepare |
|---|---|---|
| Online judge screening | Paiza, AtCoder, HackerRank | Algorithm fundamentals — arrays, hash maps, recursion, DP |
| Take-home project | Company-hosted (GitHub repo submission) | Clean code, tests, a README explaining tradeoffs |
| Live coding interview | CoderPad, Google Docs shared screen | Communicate your thought process out loud, even in English |
| System design round | Whiteboard or verbal (mid-to-senior only) | Usually skipped for new grads, common from year 2–3 onward |

A few Japan-specific notes:

- **Paiza is worth knowing specifically.** It's a Japan-native coding assessment platform many companies use for initial screening, and it ranks you on a public skill tier (S through D) that some job listings explicitly require (e.g., "Paiza rank A or above"). Building a public Paiza rank ahead of applications can shortcut screening entirely at some firms.
- **AtCoder participation is a genuine differentiator.** Many Japanese engineers, including hiring managers, are active competitive programmers. A visible AtCoder history (even modest rankings) signals technical seriousness in a way that's specific to this market.
- Live coding interviews at international-friendly companies are commonly conducted in English even when the rest of the interview process is in Japanese — confirm the language format when scheduling.

## Specialized Recruitment Agencies vs. Going Direct

For international students, using a recruiter who specializes in tech and bilingual placement is usually more effective than blind applications, especially for visa-sponsoring roles. These agencies are paid by the hiring company (typically 20–35% of first-year salary), so their service is free to you as a candidate, and they pre-screen listings for visa sponsorship, which saves enormous time.

| Agency/Platform | Focus | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| Wantedly | Startup and mega-venture listings, informal "meet first" culture | Direct networking, junior engineers |
| Green | Broad tech job board, filterable by visa sponsorship | Self-directed searching across company sizes |
| Daijob | Bilingual/international-focused listings | Candidates with moderate Japanese |
| Robert Walters Japan | Mid-to-senior tech recruiting, bilingual consultants | Candidates targeting foreign-capital or hybrid firms |
| Mynavi Global | New-graduate-specific international student support | Traditional new-grad-adjacent engineering roles |
| Tokyo Dev / Japan Dev job boards | English-first, remote-friendly and international teams | Candidates prioritizing English work environments |

A practical approach: register with two or three agencies simultaneously (there's no exclusivity requirement in most cases), and be explicit up front about your visa status, Japanese level, and target compensation. A good tech-specialist recruiter will tell you within one conversation whether your portfolio is interview-ready, which is valuable free feedback even before you formally apply anywhere.

## Visa Sponsorship and Timing Your Job Search

The relevant visa category for most IT roles is **Engineer/Specialist in Humanities/International Services**, though many companies now also reference the **Highly Skilled Professional (HSP)** visa if your points score qualifies (advanced degree, salary, age, and Japanese ability all contribute points, and HSP status can fast-track permanent residency to as little as 1–3 years).

Key logistics:

- Your college major should reasonably relate to the job (a CS, engineering, or math-adjacent degree is safest; other majors can still qualify with a clear justification, such as prior work experience or a coding bootcamp certificate)
- Visa sponsorship typically takes 1–3 months for the Certificate of Eligibility once a company commits to hiring you, so factor this into your offer timeline
- If you're graduating from a Japanese university, you can apply for **Designated Activities (job-hunting) status**, which extends your stay up to 12 months post-graduation specifically to continue job searching — a critical safety net if your search runs past graduation
- International students already in Japan have a real advantage: employers can meet you in person, and agencies can schedule interviews without coordinating cross-border logistics

Unlike the rigid shukatsu calendar, the engineer track hires close to year-round, so there's no single deadline you need to hit. Companies with active headcount will interview you in October just as readily as in April. That said, starting your portfolio and applications 6–8 months before your target start date gives you enough runway to iterate after early interview rejections, which are common and not a signal to stop.

---

The overall shape of this path is straightforward even if the execution takes real work: build something real and deployed, prove your coding ability on platforms Japanese employers actually check, and let a specialist recruiter route you toward companies that sponsor visas and hire on skill. Skip the entry-sheet essays — nobody is grading your portfolio on your handwriting.
