## Understanding the "Job-Hunting Visa": What It Actually Covers

If you're graduating from a Japanese university or vocational school this spring and still haven't landed a job offer, you don't have to leave the country. Japan's immigration system has a dedicated status precisely for this situation: the **"Designated Activities" visa for job hunting** (特定活動, *Tokutei Katsudo*), sometimes informally called the "job-hunting visa." It lets former international students who graduated (or are about to graduate) remain in Japan legally while continuing to search for full-time employment, without needing a company to sponsor a Work visa yet.

This status is not automatic — you must apply for it before your Student visa expires, and it comes with real conditions: limited part-time work hours, mandatory proof of active job-hunting activity, and a hard cap on how long you can stay under this category. Understanding the mechanics now, while you still have time, is the difference between a smooth transition and a rushed scramble in your final semester.

## Who Qualifies and When to Apply

Not every student is automatically eligible. Immigration officers generally look for the following before granting this status:

- You have graduated (or will imminently graduate) from a Japanese university, junior college, professional training college (*senmon gakko*), or graduate school.
- You received a **letter of recommendation from your school's career center** confirming you are a legitimate job-seeker and were enrolled in good standing.
- You have a documented history of job-hunting activity — job fair attendance, entries on Rikunabi/Mynavi, entry sheets submitted, or interview records.
- You do not have a criminal record and have complied with your Student visa conditions (including the 28-hour/week part-time work cap).

**Timing matters.** Most students apply for this Designated Activities status either:

1. At the point their Student visa is close to expiring but graduation hasn't yet led to a job offer, or
2. Immediately after graduation, if they were unable to secure employment before commencement.

Apply at your regional Immigration Services Agency office **before your current Student visa expires** — ideally 1–2 months ahead, since processing can take several weeks and offices get busy during the March–April graduation season.

## Required Documents Checklist

Documentation requirements vary slightly by immigration office, but the following list covers what nearly every applicant needs to submit.

| Document | Who Provides It | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Application for Change of Status of Residence | You | Standard immigration form, available at the Immigration Bureau or online |
| Letter of recommendation (推薦状) | University/school career center | Must confirm graduation status and job-seeking intent |
| Certificate of graduation or expected graduation | University/school | Original or certified copy |
| Academic transcript | University/school | Some offices request this to verify enrollment history |
| Job-hunting activity report | You | List of companies applied to, job fairs attended, interview dates |
| Passport and Residence Card | You | Original, for in-person verification |
| Photo (3cm x 4cm) | You | Taken within the last 3 months |
| Proof of financial support | You (or sponsor) | Bank statements, savings, or a guarantor's proof of income |
| Resume/CV (履歴書) | You | Japanese-style rirekisho is often preferred |

Bring both originals and photocopies. Missing the recommendation letter is the single most common reason applications get delayed — request it from your career office well before your final semester ends, since staff processing times pile up around graduation.

## The 6-Month Extension, Explained

This is the heart of the system. Once granted, the Designated Activities (job-hunting) status is issued in **6-month increments**, and it can be **renewed once**, giving you a **maximum total of 12 months** to continue searching after your original graduation-linked eligibility begins.

Here's how the two phases typically break down:

| Phase | Duration | What's Required to Renew |
|---|---|---|
| Initial grant | 6 months | Proof of enrollment/graduation, career center recommendation, job-hunting plan |
| First renewal | +6 months (12 months total) | Evidence of continued, active job search during the first 6 months (interviews, applications, job fair attendance) |
| After 12 months | No further renewal under this category | Must switch to Work visa (if hired), leave Japan, or explore another status if eligible |

A critical detail: renewal is **not guaranteed**. Immigration officers expect to see a genuine, documented effort — a folder of rejection emails and interview invitations is actually a stronger renewal case than an empty activity log. If your file shows minimal effort (few or no applications, no interviews), the renewal can be denied.

**Practical tip:** Keep a running log from day one — spreadsheet columns for company name, application date, response, and interview date. This becomes your evidence packet for the 6-month renewal interview.

## Work Restrictions While You Search

You are allowed to work part-time under this status, but the rules mirror — and in some ways tighten — what you had as a student.

- You must separately apply for a **"Permission to Engage in Activity Other Than That Permitted by the Status of Residence Previously Granted"** (資格外活動許可) if you want to do any part-time work at all. It is not automatic just because you had one as a student — confirm it's still valid or reapply.
- The **28-hours-per-week cap** still applies (up to 40 hours/week during long school breaks does *not* apply anymore, since you're no longer enrolled).
- Work must not be in the adult entertainment industry (this restriction is strictly enforced, as with the Student visa).
- Income from part-time work should be treated as supplementary — this status does not permit full-time employment. Once you accept a full-time job offer, you must switch your status before starting work.

If a company offers you a position while you're on this visa, you cannot simply start working. You need to file a **Change of Status of Residence** application to convert to the appropriate Work visa (e.g., Engineer/Specialist in Humanities/International Services, or another category matching your job) before your first day.

## City-by-City Differences in Processing

While the national rules are uniform, practical experience differs depending on where you apply, largely due to office volume and appointment systems.

| Region | Typical Wait Time | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Tokyo (Shinagawa) | 4–8 weeks | Highest volume nationally; book online appointment slots early, especially Feb–April |
| Osaka | 3–6 weeks | Slightly less congested; walk-ins possible on some days but expect long lines |
| Nagoya | 2–4 weeks | Moderate volume |
| Fukuoka | 2–4 weeks | Popular with students from Kyushu universities; relatively fast processing |
| Sapporo/Sendai (regional) | 1–3 weeks | Lower volume, often faster turnaround |

If you're in Tokyo, plan your visit around the graduation rush: late February through April sees the heaviest congestion as thousands of students apply simultaneously. Applying slightly earlier (as soon as your career center issues the recommendation letter) can save weeks of waiting.

## What Happens If You Reach the 12-Month Limit

If you've used both the initial 6 months and the renewal and still haven't secured a job, your options narrow considerably:

- **Return to your home country** and reapply for a Work visa later if a Japanese employer sponsors you from abroad.
- **Switch to a different visa category** if you qualify — for example, a Working Holiday visa (if your nationality is eligible and you haven't used it already), a Dependent visa (if you have a qualifying family relationship), or further study (returning to school resets the clock but is a significant commitment).
- **Depart and re-enter** on a Short-Term Stay visa isn't a real workaround — it doesn't permit job hunting or work activities and immigration officers scrutinize repeat short-term entries closely.

The healthiest approach is to treat the 12-month ceiling as a hard deadline from day one, not a cushion. Many successful job-seekers secure offers within the first 6 months precisely because they know the clock is running and prioritize applications immediately after graduation rather than easing into the search.

## Making the Most of Your Search Window

A few practices consistently correlate with students securing offers before their status runs out:

- **Register with a specialized job agency early** — services like Wahl Media Career, Daijob, or CareerCross focus on foreign talent and often move faster than general job boards.
- **Leverage your university's alumni network** — many career centers maintain lists of alumni working at companies known for hiring international graduates.
- **Attend gaikokujin (foreigner-focused) job fairs**, which are held regularly in Tokyo and Osaka and are far more efficient than cold-applying to companies unfamiliar with visa sponsorship.
- **Target companies that already sponsor visas** — check job listings explicitly, since not every Japanese employer is experienced with the sponsorship process, and this can slow down your Change of Status application even after you're hired.

This visa exists precisely to give you breathing room — use it deliberately, document everything, and treat the 6-month mark as your first checkpoint, not your deadline.
