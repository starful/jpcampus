## Understanding the 28-Hour Rule: What It Actually Covers

If you're holding a Student visa (留学生, "ryugakusei") in Japan, you cannot legally work part-time at all unless you first apply for and receive **permission to engage in activity other than that permitted under the status of residence previously granted** (資格外活動許可, *shikakugai katsudo kyoka*). Once approved, the core rule is simple on paper but trips up thousands of students every year:

- **Maximum 28 hours per week** during academic terms, including vacation periods that fall within a term
- The 28-hour cap is calculated on a **rolling weekly basis**, not monthly or per-shift
- This limit applies **across all jobs combined** — if you work at a convenience store for 18 hours and tutor for 12 hours in the same week, you've already exceeded the cap by 2 hours
- The "week" is generally interpreted as any 7-day period, so Immigration can look at overlapping weeks when reviewing your timesheets, not just Monday-to-Sunday blocks

A common point of confusion: the 28-hour limit is a **ceiling**, not an entitlement. Your institution or employer does not guarantee you 28 hours — it's simply the maximum you're allowed to work without violating your visa conditions.

| Student Visa Type | Weekly Hour Limit (Term) | Full-Time Allowance (Long Breaks) |
|---|---|---|
| University (undergraduate/graduate) | 28 hours | Up to 40 hours/week (8 hrs/day) |
| Japanese Language School | 28 hours | Up to 40 hours/week (8 hrs/day) |
| Vocational School (Senshu Gakko) | 28 hours | Up to 40 hours/week (8 hrs/day) |
| Pre-college / Prep course students | 28 hours | Up to 40 hours/week (8 hrs/day) |

## Long Breaks: When You Can Legally Work Full-Time

During officially designated long vacations — typically **summer break, winter break, and spring break** as defined by your school's academic calendar — the 28-hour weekly cap is lifted, and you may work up to **8 hours per day**, effectively full-time.

However, this exception comes with important conditions:

- The break must be an **official, school-recognized vacation period** — not just a week where you personally have no classes
- You still need **valid shikakugai katsudo kyoka** permission; the break doesn't grant you work rights, it only raises the hour ceiling
- Some schools require students to **submit a vacation schedule** or get a letter confirming the break dates, which you may need to show an employer or Immigration
- If you are **on a leave of absence (kyugaku)** rather than enrolled and actively studying, you are generally **not** eligible for the long-break exception — you're treated as not attending school, which creates separate status problems entirely
- Graduation does **not** count as a break — once you've completed your program, your eligibility to work under student permissions typically ends, even if your visa hasn't expired yet

## Jobs You're Allowed to Do vs. Jobs That Are Off-Limits

Not all part-time work is treated equally under Japanese immigration law. Certain industries are categorically prohibited for Student visa holders, regardless of how many hours you'd work.

| Category | Status | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Permitted | ✅ Allowed (within hour limits) | Convenience stores, restaurants/cafes, retail, warehouse/logistics, office assistant work, language tutoring, IT/web internships, translation work |
| Conditionally restricted | ⚠️ Allowed but regulated | Factory/manufacturing work (must not be the "principal" nature of adult entertainment-adjacent industries), delivery gig work (tax/insurance implications) |
| Prohibited | 🚫 Never allowed | Hostess/host clubs, bars requiring customer companionship, adult entertainment venues (fuzoku), pachinko parlors, mahjong parlors, other establishments under the Entertainment Business Law (風俗営業) |

The legal basis for the prohibited category is Japan's **Entertainment Business Law (風俗営業等取締法)**. Immigration explicitly bars student visa holders from working in or around these establishments — this includes cleaning, kitchen, or administrative jobs located *inside* these venues, even if the job itself seems harmless. A student who takes a dishwashing job at a venue technically licensed as a "snack bar" with hostess service can still be found in violation, because the classification of the business matters more than the specific duties performed.

**Red flags that a job offer may be illegal for students:**

- Pay significantly above market rate for unskilled work (often a sign of unregistered or exploitative employment)
- Job ads specifically targeting "foreign students" for nightlife-adjacent positions
- Employers who don't ask to see your residence card or work permit at all
- Cash-only payment with no pay stub or labor contract

## How Immigration Actually Catches Hour Violations

Many students assume enforcement is rare or random. In practice, the Immigration Services Agency (出入国在留管理庁, ISA) has multiple structured ways to detect violations, and cross-checking has become more sophisticated in recent years.

1. **Visa renewal and status review**: Every time you apply for an Extension of Period of Stay or Change of Status, Immigration reviews your work history, often requesting pay slips (給与明細) covering the prior months.
2. **Employer reporting obligations**: Japanese employers hiring foreign nationals must submit notifications to Hello Work (public employment security office) regarding hiring and termination. These records are cross-referenced with Immigration data.
3. **Tax and social insurance records**: Resident tax filings and health insurance enrollment can reveal income inconsistent with declared work hours.
4. **School attendance reporting**: Schools are required to report students with poor attendance to Immigration. A student working excessive hours often has an attendance drop that triggers a separate review — the two issues frequently surface together.
5. **Workplace inspections and tip-offs**: Labor Standards Inspection Offices occasionally investigate workplaces, and discrepancies in reported hours for foreign staff can be flagged to Immigration.
6. **Residence card scanning**: Employers hiring part-time staff must verify and record residence card details, including the work permission sticker on the back. These records can be audited.

Because pay slips are the most common document requested, keeping personal records — even a simple spreadsheet of hours worked per week across all jobs — is one of the most practical habits a student can build.

## What Happens If You Exceed the Limit

The consequences scale with the severity and frequency of the violation, but even a single documented overage can cause problems.

| Violation Severity | Typical Consequence |
|---|---|
| Minor, one-time overage (e.g., 30 hours in one week) | Warning noted at visa renewal; possible request for explanation letter |
| Repeated pattern of overage over months | Denial or delay of visa extension; shortened renewal period (e.g., 1 year instead of 2) |
| Working in a prohibited industry | Immediate risk of visa revocation, regardless of hours worked |
| Severe/sustained violation combined with poor attendance | Status revocation, deportation order, re-entry ban (typically 1–10 years depending on circumstances) |
| Working without any shikakugai katsudo kyoka at all | Treated as illegal employment; criminal liability possible under the Immigration Control Act |

It's worth noting that **employers can also face penalties** for knowingly hiring students beyond legal hours or without proper permission, under Article 73-2 of the Immigration Control Act. This is why legitimate employers are often strict about requesting your residence card and permission sticker before scheduling shifts — if your favorite part-time job suddenly asks for updated documentation, it's a compliance step, not suspicion of you personally.

## Real-World Scenarios Students Often Get Wrong

**Scenario 1: The "two jobs, under 28 hours each" trap**
A student works 20 hours at a restaurant and 15 hours tutoring, believing each job is "within limits." Combined, that's 35 hours — a clear violation. Always total your hours across every job, every week.

**Scenario 2: Unpaid overtime or "off the clock" extra hours**
An employer asks a student to stay an extra hour unpaid during a busy shift. Even unpaid time worked counts toward the 28-hour cap under immigration interpretation, because the restriction is about activity, not compensation.

**Scenario 3: Working during a personal gap week, not an official break**
A student's classes happen to be light one week due to exam scheduling, so they work 35 hours, assuming it's fine since "school is basically on break." Unless the school calendar formally designates that week as a vacation period, the 28-hour cap still applies.

**Scenario 4: Switching schools or falling into part-time/leave status**
A student transfers between language schools or takes a leave of absence while continuing to work the same part-time job. Work permission is tied to active enrollment status; a gap in enrollment can invalidate your ability to legally work during that period, even if your residence card hasn't expired.

**Scenario 5: Freelance or gig platform work**
A student picks up food delivery gig work assuming it's informal and therefore unregulated. Gig income still counts toward the 28-hour cap and must still be declared, and the lack of a standard employer relationship doesn't exempt it from scrutiny — it can actually make hour-tracking harder and riskier to prove compliance.

## Staying Compliant Without Sacrificing Income

Balancing finances and visa safety doesn't have to mean guesswork. A few habits make a meaningful difference:

- Keep a personal weekly log of hours across **all** jobs, updated in real time rather than reconstructed later
- Ask your school's student affairs office for **official vacation period dates** in writing each term
- Before accepting any new part-time offer, confirm the business type isn't classified under the Entertainment Business Law
- Request pay slips every month and store them — you'll likely need several months' worth at your next visa renewal
- If you're ever unsure whether a vacation week qualifies for the 40-hour exception, ask your school directly rather than assuming

Part-time work is a normal and valuable part of student life in Japan — it helps with living costs and builds real workplace experience. The system isn't designed to make this hard; it's designed to keep international students in legitimate, safe employment while protecting their primary purpose of being in Japan: full-time study. Respecting the hour limits and job restrictions isn't just about avoiding penalties — it's what keeps your path toward future visas, including work visas after graduation, clean and uncomplicated.
